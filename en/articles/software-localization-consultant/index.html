Use cases

Software localization consultant: working with foreign product teams

A Californian fitness app wants to launch an Italian version; a German business-software platform must localise its interface, error messages and documentation. Translating an interface is not like translating a book: strings are short, without context, with variables and placeholders. A localization consultant knows the product, respects the available space and knows when a literal translation goes wrong.

Typical activities

  • String translation with resource files in various formats.
  • Building the glossary and style guide, for instance how to address the user (formal or informal).
  • Linguistic review and QA directly in the app, with screenshots.
  • Localizing documentation, help articles, emails and notifications.
  • Cultural adaptation: date formats, currencies, examples that make sense in Italy.
  • Pseudo-localization and testing of text length.

Deliverables are translated files, a glossary, QA reports and reporting of linguistic bugs.

Where problems arise

  1. Strings without context: is “Home” a menu or a house? Ask for screenshots and comments.
  2. Placeholders and plurals: variable errors break the interface. A technical check is needed.
  3. Continuous updates: software changes every week; the work is recurring, with irregular volumes.
  4. Translation memories: who owns them? Write it down.
  5. Per-word rates with discounts for repetitions: understand how they are calculated.

Quality and delivery times

Ask for access to a test build or to screenshots with strings in context, and report ambiguities in a single ordered list. Agree an update calendar: often the heaviest work is not the first translation but chasing continuous changes. Check how plurals and gender are handled, which in Italian require different forms from English. A shared glossary and a short style guide, updated with each release, reduce inconsistencies and revisions.

Example: an app in Italian

A fictional example. Imagine Chiara, a translator specialising in software. A Californian company asks her to localize a fitness app into Italian: eight thousand words of interface, a glossary, a QA pass on the app and then monthly updates. Per-word rate for strings, day rate for QA. Through the cooperative, Tesla3000 signs a contract with both rates; Chiara logs volumes and days on the platform and receives a monthly payslip covering the activities done. The client receives a single invoice in dollars.

How it works with the cooperative

  1. Send the project: volumes, file format, planned updates.
  2. Membership with a share of capital refundable on exit (Law 142/2001).
  3. Contract: per-word and day rates, translation memories, confidentiality.
  4. Platform: activities, deliveries, tool expenses, signing.
  5. Invoicing: the non-EU client as a B2B service, rules verified case by case.
  6. Payslip with INPS, INAIL and professional liability included; advance if collection is late.

Cooperative or VAT number

Many translators work with a VAT number under the flat-rate regime (85,000 euro threshold, 15% substitute tax, 5% for the first five years with requirements), with Separate Management contributions and several clients in parallel: efficient for those with many clients. In the cooperative, work for a few continuous clients becomes a regular payslip, with holidays and sick leave under the employment relationship. Rules on intellectual works and copyright must be checked case by case.

Frequently asked questions

Is pay per word or per hour?

Both exist. For interface strings a per-word rate is common; for QA and linguistic consulting, an hourly or day rate.

Can I use machine translation tools?

It depends on the client's rules. Declare it and agree it, also for confidentiality of the text.

Who maintains the glossary?

Decide it: the product glossary belongs to the client, but the consultant can propose terms and keep them updated during the assignment.
