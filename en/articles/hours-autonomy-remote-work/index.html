Hours, autonomy and organising remote work

People working remotely for a foreign client are often described as “free”: no office, no clocking in, no boss passing behind the desk. Reality is more nuanced. Autonomy exists, but it moves within a framework: contract, client expectations, time zones. Good organisation makes the difference between real autonomy and days without boundaries.

Autonomy and employment: how they fit together

In a cooperative the member often has an employment contract. That does not mean minute-by-minute control of hours: it means there are rules on length of work, rest, holiday and attendance. The client defines results and availability; the member organises the work to achieve them. Exact boundaries depend on contract, agreement and regulation.

Three levels of rules

  1. Employment contract: overall hours, any time bands, holiday and leave.
  2. Agreement with the client: required availability, meetings, response times.
  3. Internal regulation: how hours are recorded and reported.

When in doubt, the most specific text applies, but it is always best to ask the cooperative.

Time zones

This is the most practical issue. With clients in the Americas or Asia, meetings fall at unusual hours. Some useful rules:

  • agree an overlap window (say two to three hours) in which you are reachable;
  • use asynchronous communication for everything else: documents, videos, clear messages;
  • write in the contract or agreement who bears non-standard hours;
  • do not get dragged into unlimited availability: it is the commonest cause of exhaustion.

Routines that work

  • Fixed base hours, even if flexible, to keep rhythm.
  • Deep-work blocks without notifications.
  • A real break mid-day.
  • Daily close: write a summary and note what comes next.
  • Dedicated physical space, even a small one.
  • Immediate recording of hours and activities on the platform.

Timesheets and reporting

Recording activities and hours serves three purposes: calculating the payslip correctly, documenting work for the client, and protecting you in case of disputes. Doing it daily takes five minutes; doing it at month end can take sixty and produce errors.

Example: Alessio, graphic designer

A fictional example: Alessio works for a Toronto agency. He agrees with the client an overlap window from 3 pm to 6 pm Italian time, and uses mornings for solo work. Friday afternoon is for the weekly delivery. He logs hours every evening. When the client proposes a meeting at 11 pm Italian time, Alessio asks to move it and offers a video recording. The client accepts. Without agreed rules, night meetings would have become habitual.

How it works with the cooperative

  1. Setting the schedule. The employment contract and agreement state commitment and availability.
  2. Platform. You record hours and activities.
  3. Support. Tesla3000 answers questions on hours, holiday and leave via WhatsApp or email.
  4. Periodic review. Actual workload is compared with the planned one.
  5. Adjustments. If commitment changes, the contract is updated.

What to check

  • Rules on maximum hours, rest, night and holiday work.
  • How to request holiday and leave.
  • Notice for out-of-hours meetings.
  • Equipment and connectivity: who provides them.

Frequently asked questions

Who decides my hours?

They depend on the employment contract, the regulation and agreements with the client. There is often flexibility, but with availability windows or agreed meetings.

Do I have to record my hours?

Yes, on the cooperative's platform: it is needed for payroll, client reporting and protections. The method is set by the regulation.

Can I work at night or on weekends?

It depends on the contract and applicable rules on hours, rest and overtime. Check with the cooperative before organising yourself that way.
