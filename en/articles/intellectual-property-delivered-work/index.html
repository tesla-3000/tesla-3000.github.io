Intellectual property: who owns the delivered work

This is among the most underestimated topics in dealings with foreign clients: when you deliver software, text, graphics or a model, who owns it? The answer depends on the contract, the type of work and the country. This guide gives a general picture and the questions to ask, without claiming to be legal advice.

Three components to distinguish

Pre-existing material

What you had before the assignment: personal libraries, models, templates, methods. Normally it stays yours, unless a clause says otherwise.

Results developed for the client

The work produced under the agreement. In very many commercial contracts the client obtains rights of use or ownership. The exact wording varies: assignment of rights, exclusive licence, “work made for hire”.

General tools and knowledge

Skills, techniques, accumulated experience. They are not the client’s property; it matters that the contract does not try to restrict them.

What changes with the cooperative

In the cooperative model, the commercial contract is signed by the cooperative with the client, while the member works under a contract with the cooperative. IP clauses must therefore be consistent at two levels: between client and cooperative, and between cooperative and member. The member should know that the rules in the employment contract and internal regulation determine how results produced in the activity are attributed. The concrete treatment depends on these documents and on the applicable law, so it must be checked case by case.

Questions to ask before signing

  • Does the contract speak of assignment of rights or a licence? Exclusive or not?
  • Is there a territory and a duration?
  • Does the assignment cover only delivered work or also drafts and intermediate versions?
  • What happens to pre-existing material I incorporate in the work?
  • Can I include the work in my portfolio? With what limits?
  • What happens if the client does not pay: do rights pass anyway?

The case of creative works

Text, images, music, photographs and similar may be protected by copyright, which includes moral and economic rights with their own rules. Contractual solutions are more delicate, and specific advice is recommended. For activities involving copyright or artistic works, a standard agreement formula may not be enough.

Example: Paola, illustrator

A fictional example: Paola illustrates books for a Canadian publisher. In the draft agreement she reads a clause assigning the client “all materials produced, including pre-existing ones”. She flags the problem: her digital brushes and original textures, created years earlier, must not be assigned. With the cooperative she proposes a change: assignment of delivered results only, a licence to use incorporated pre-existing elements, and the right to show the work in her portfolio after publication. The client accepts with minor changes. Another work or client may require different solutions.

How it works with the cooperative

  1. Flagging. You send the draft contract to Tesla3000 and indicate any pre-existing material.
  2. Clause analysis. The cooperative identifies critical points.
  3. Proposal to the client. Precise, reasoned changes are suggested.
  4. Alignment with your contract. The rules between cooperative and member must be consistent.
  5. Filing. Documents remain accessible on the platform.

What to check

  • Assignment and licence clauses, duration and territory.
  • Right to use the work in your portfolio.
  • Treatment of third-party materials and open-source licences.
  • Whether specific legal advice is needed, especially for creative or patentable works.

Frequently asked questions

Do the rights to my work always go to the client?

It depends on the contract, the type of work and the applicable law. In many agreements the client acquires rights in delivered work, but details must be read case by case.

Can I reuse tools and materials I already had?

Often yes, if the contract distinguishes pre-existing from new material. It is better to list them in the agreement to avoid doubt.

Do creative works have special rules?

Yes: copyright and artistic works have specific rules and must be checked carefully, ideally with an adviser.
