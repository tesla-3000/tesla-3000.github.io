Software developer with a US client: how to work legally

It happens often: an Italian developer is noticed by a US startup, the collaboration goes well, and a near full-time engagement is offered. The client asks for one simple thing, a monthly invoice. For someone without a VAT number, this is where things stall. With Tesla3000 there is another route: working as a worker-member of a cooperative, with an employment contract and a payslip.

What US clients usually expect

American companies hiring external developers think in terms of “contractors”: a vendor that invoices regularly, often hourly or monthly, under a services agreement (an MSA plus a statement of work). They typically ask for:

  • a clearly identifiable entity that invoices on time;
  • confidentiality and assignment of rights on the code produced;
  • overlap with part of the US working day;
  • payment in dollars by international transfer.

The cooperative covers all of this: it is the vendor, it signs the commercial agreement and it invoices. For B2B services to non-EU clients, generic services are normally not subject to Italian VAT, though each case is checked individually.

Employment contract instead of a VAT number

Under Italian Law 142/2001, a worker-member has, besides the membership relationship, a further and distinct work relationship with the cooperative, which can be an employment contract. Social security (INPS) is paid by the cooperative, there is INAIL accident insurance, and paid leave, sick leave, parental leave and severance (TFR) accrue. If you lose your job involuntarily, unemployment benefit (NASpI) may be available under the legal conditions. You get a monthly payslip and an annual tax certificate, with no bookkeeping, no e-invoicing and no tax returns to manage on the business side.

The flat-rate VAT regime remains a valid option, especially with several clients and healthy margins, but you pay your own contributions and handle the paperwork. With a single client and a wish for employee protections, the cooperative is worth comparing.

Typical pitfalls in software work

  • Intellectual property. A US client will want assignment of the code. The clause belongs in the commercial agreement, not in a private side deal.
  • NDAs and access. Repository and environment access should be traceable.
  • Hours versus milestones. Hourly billing needs a monthly activity summary; milestone billing depends on client acceptance, which affects when money arrives.
  • Time zones. Agree availability windows from day one.

Example

Example: imagine Marco, a 32-year-old backend developer in Bologna working for a startup in Austin. The client wants 160 hours a month for six months, renewable. Marco does not want a VAT number for a single client. The cooperative signs the commercial agreement with the startup (scope, term, hourly rate, code assignment, confidentiality) and engages Marco as a worker-member. Each month he logs hours on the platform, the client approves them, the cooperative invoices in dollars and, after collection, issues his payslip. Name and details are fictional.

How it works with the cooperative

  1. Intro call. You describe the project: client, term, rate, payment terms.
  2. Joining. You become a member by subscribing a share of capital, refundable on exit according to the bylaws.
  3. Commercial agreement. The cooperative contracts with the foreign client.
  4. Employment contract. You receive yours, in line with the cooperative’s internal regulation.
  5. Monthly activity. You record time and activities on the platform; expenses are reimbursed.
  6. Invoice and payslip. The cooperative invoices, collects (also in foreign currency) and pays you through payroll. A payment advance from a dedicated fund is available if needed.

The package includes INAIL cover and professional liability insurance, plus WhatsApp and email support.

What to check first

If you live, or plan to live, abroad, tax residence and double taxation treaties must be assessed case by case; we cannot promise a uniform outcome. If the client asks for exclusivity or very tight constraints, we check together that the arrangement fits the type of contract.

Checklist before signing

Before accepting the offer, prepare these points: the text of the MSA or statement of work with its clauses on code ownership and confidentiality; the hourly or monthly rate and the currency; the invoicing calendar (usually month end, with payment at 15, 30 or 45 days); overlap hours with the US team; the policy on leave and American public holidays, which differ from ours. If the client proposes a trial period, write down how long it lasts and how it is paid. The clearer these points are from the start, the fewer arguments you will have mid-project. Share them with us and we turn them into clauses of the agreement.

If you have an offer from a US client and want to know whether this fits, contact us and we will give you a concrete assessment.

Frequently asked questions

Does the US client sign a contract with me?

No. The commercial contract is signed between the client and the cooperative. As a worker-member you have an employment relationship with the cooperative and agree rate and working terms with it.

Can I be paid in dollars?

The cooperative can invoice and collect in foreign currency. Your pay on the payslip is in euro; conversion terms are set out in the commercial agreement.

How is the income taxed?

Employment income is subject to progressive personal income tax (IRPEF) withheld at source with year-end adjustment. Special situations, such as living abroad, are assessed case by case.
