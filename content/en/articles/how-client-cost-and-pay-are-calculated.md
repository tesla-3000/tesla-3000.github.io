One question always comes up: "if I invoice X for my work, how much do I receive?" The honest answer is that there is no universal coefficient: it depends on contract, job classification and expenses. There is, however, a logic, and understanding it lets you do realistic sums without being promised figures.

## The starting point: the price of the service

You start from the price of your service: what the project, phase or monthly retainer you offer the client is worth on the market. You set it, based on the value of the result and prices in your field. This is the figure that appears in the contract and on the invoice.

## How revenue is split

From revenue (excluding VAT) the **cooperative's service fee** is deducted (see [pricing](/en/pricing/)). The rest is your **activity budget**, which covers:

- **reimbursable expenses** related to the work;
- **employer social security contributions** (INPS) and **workplace accident insurance** (INAIL);
- **accruals** for holiday, leave, any additional monthly payments and **severance (TFR)**;
- your **gross pay**, per contract and job classification.

The service fee covers administrative management, platform, invoicing and collection, professional liability insurance, support and training: the part you would otherwise bear yourself through an accountant, software and policies.

## From gross to net

From gross pay you get to net by deducting the worker's own contributions and applying progressive income tax with withholding at source and year-end adjustment, plus regional and municipal surtaxes. Net pay therefore also depends on deductions and family situation. That is why it is right to speak of a personalised estimate, not a fixed percentage.

## Setting a sustainable price

To see whether a price works, estimate how much work the service takes and how much budget you need for the pay you want, plus expenses and contributions. A margin for periods without projects (holidays, illness, gaps) should be included realistically. If the numbers do not add up, you revise the scope or the price, not your pay.

## Example: Paolo, backend developer

A fictional example, with deliberately unreal numbers: Paolo has to quote for a payment API split into three milestones. He estimates the work for each phase, looks at market prices for similar projects and sets a price per milestone. He then asks the cooperative for a simulation: the service fee is deducted from the expected revenue and he sees the resulting net pay with his personal situation. If he is not satisfied, he revises the price before sending the offer, rather than relying on generic gross-to-net ratios.
