A game artist creates characters, environments, props, textures, rigs and animations; a 3D animator brings characters and objects to life for games, series, ads and films. Foreign studios, especially indie and animation ones, often hire external artists for deadline-driven projects. {{< brand >}} organises the work relationship and payments.

## How a studio works with external artists

Production follows a **pipeline**: concept, modelling, sculpting (ZBrush), retopology, UVs, texturing (Substance), rigging, animation, engine integration (Unity, Unreal), optimisation. Each asset goes through review by the art director and the tech artist. Pay is per **asset** (with rates for complexity), per **day** or per **milestone**. Timing depends on approvers, so delayed feedback slows everything. Agree in the contract:

- asset list and quality level (polygon budget, texture resolution);
- revision rounds and feedback times;
- delivery formats and naming conventions;
- who supplies software licences and references.

## Rights and confidentiality

Models, textures and animations are protected works. The studio usually asks for full assignment of work produced for the project; copyright has specific rules and must be checked case by case. NDAs are very common: they also limit portfolio use until the game is public.

## The cooperative's role

The cooperative signs the agreement with the studio and invoices. For EU clients the reverse charge normally applies (art. 7-ter of decree 633/72); for non-EU clients generic B2B services are normally outside Italian VAT: checked case by case. As a worker-member (Italian Law 142/2001) you have a work relationship with INPS contributions, INAIL and, if employment, paid leave, sick leave, severance and a payslip. Useful with long engagements where you work continuously for one studio. Expense reports cover agreed travel and purchases.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Revisions without end.** Without limits, the asset is never "finished".
- **Scope and polygon budget.** Spec changes late in the work cost time.
- **Hardware.** Rendering and simulation need powerful machines: who pays for wear?
- **Crunch.** Release deadlines can produce hour peaks: regulate them in the contract.
- **Banned portfolio.** Long NDAs can block your visibility for years.

## How it works with the cooperative

1. **Intro call.** You present project, assets, milestones, rate.
2. **Joining.** Share of capital, refundable on exit under the bylaws.
3. **Agreement.** Contract with the studio, with assignment, NDA and portfolio.
4. **Activity.** Days, deliveries and expenses on the platform.
5. **Invoicing.** The cooperative invoices and collects, also in foreign currency.
6. **Payslip.** Monthly pay and documents online; fund advance if needed.

INAIL, professional liability insurance, training and the members' community are included.

## Compared with a VAT number

The flat-rate regime (revenue up to 85,000 euro, 15% substitute tax, 5% for the first five years if conditions are met) is common among freelance artists: autonomy and low costs, but contributions and paperwork are yours. The cooperative is preferable for those who want a stable relationship and protections.

## Checklist before signing

Before accepting, ask for: concept art and style references; the polygon budget and technical specs; the engine and pipeline (formats, naming, versioning); the asset list and priority; who approves and in what time. Propose a paid test asset to validate quality and workflow. Keep a backup of working files and a log of versions delivered. Agree whether you can use references and tools like Substance or ZBrush under your own or the client's licences. Anything that affects timing goes in the agreement.
