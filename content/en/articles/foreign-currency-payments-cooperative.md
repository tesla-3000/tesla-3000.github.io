Working for foreign clients often means being paid in different currencies: US dollars, pounds, Swiss francs, Swedish crowns. For a freelancer this means foreign-currency accounts or payment platforms, fees, exchange-rate monitoring and reconciliation. With {{< brand >}} this part is handled by the cooperative.

## What the cooperative does

The cooperative signs the commercial contract with the foreign client and invoices the service, in foreign currency where agreed. It receives payment into its own accounts, manages the exchange and any charges, and reconciles the receipt with the invoice. You deal with none of it: you receive your pay in euro through your payslip, under the employment relationship defined.

That does not mean exchange rate risk disappears by magic: it must be clearly settled in the engagement. You can, for example, set the rate in euro, or in foreign currency with an adjustment clause, or apply a reference exchange rate at a given date.

## Example: Giacomo and the dollars

Example: imagine Giacomo, a technical consultant working for a company in Austin. Before, he invoiced in dollars via a payment platform, paying fees and losing hours reconciling amounts. In the cooperative, the engagement provides a daily rate in dollars; the cooperative invoices, collects and manages the exchange. Giacomo logs the days worked, and each month his pay in euro is calculated under the agreed employment relationship. If the dollar moves a lot, the clause in the engagement determines how that is accounted for.

## How it works with the cooperative

1. **Currency definition.** In the engagement we state which currency to invoice in and how exchange rate changes are handled.
2. **Invoicing.** The cooperative issues the invoice to the client, with VAT treatment assessed case by case (B2B services: reverse charge for the EU, generally not subject to Italian VAT for non-EU).
3. **Collection.** Payment arrives in the cooperative's accounts.
4. **Exchange and charges.** Handled by the cooperative within what was agreed.
5. **Pay.** You receive a payslip in euro; logged days form the basis of calculation.
6. **Advance.** If the client pays slowly, an advance through the fund can be considered.

## Always clarify

**Payment terms.** Foreign clients often pay at 30, 60 or more days: agree them in the contract and check with us how they fit your monthly pay.

**International payments.** SEPA transfers, SWIFT, platforms: each channel has different timing and costs. The client must be informed in advance of the correct payment details.

**Documentation.** Invoices, contracts and payment confirmations are tracked on the platform, useful in case of checks or disputes.

**If you live abroad.** Your tax residence and double taxation treaties must be assessed separately: we cannot take any treatment for granted.

We promise no net amounts or exchange rates: they depend on the market and your situation.

## When the client pays late or disputes

A foreign-currency receipt can get complicated if the client pays late, disputes the amount or pays with a different charge than expected. In those cases the cooperative, as contract holder, handles reminders and clarifications while you keep working. Keeping activity records on the platform and written approvals of deliveries helps document what is due. For more delicate situations, WhatsApp support gives you a quick point of contact.

## A practical checklist

Before the first invoice, check four things with the client: the invoicing currency, the payment term, the bank details to use and the person in their accounts office who will receive the invoice. Ask them to state the purchase order number if they use one, because many foreign companies refuse to pay invoices without it. Keep these details in the engagement record so that any later reminder can be precise and quick, and so that nothing depends on one person's memory.
