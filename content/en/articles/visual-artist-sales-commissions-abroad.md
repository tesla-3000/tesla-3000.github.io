For a visual artist, income has several faces: selling works to collectors and galleries, commissions for murals, illustrations or installations, workshops, sometimes licences. When clients are abroad, the rules multiply. Understanding what fits into working with {{< brand >}} and what does not is the first step.

## Telling three activities apart

**Commission as paid work.** A foreign business asks you to paint a mural, produce a set of illustrations or art-direct an installation. It is a service with defined timing, fee and delivery: it suits being handled as a cooperative engagement, with an employment relationship and payslips.

**Sale of artworks.** Selling an already-made work follows a different regime. We do not treat it as paid work, and it should be checked case by case with an adviser.

**Copyright and licences.** These are governed by copyright law and specific contracts. In the engagement we state clearly what is assigned or licensed to the client, for how long and in which territories.

## Example: Elena and the mural in Vienna

Example: imagine Elena, a painter, who receives a request from a Vienna restaurant to paint a mural in two weeks. Elena also sells paintings through a gallery, but for the mural she wants a clear framework. She joins the cooperative.

The cooperative signs the commercial contract with the venue: description of the work, timing, fee, materials, rules on image use and rights. Elena works on site, logs her days on the platform and claims materials and travel. She receives a payslip and has INAIL coverage, an important point when working on scaffolding.

## How it works with the cooperative

1. **Describe the commission.** You tell us the client, place, timing and nature of the work.
2. **Check the type of activity.** We determine what is paid work manageable in the cooperative and what stays outside.
3. **Membership.** You become a member with a share capital quota.
4. **Commercial contract.** It covers subject, fee, delivery and copyright clauses shared with you.
5. **Employment relationship.** The cooperative engages you for the length of the commission.
6. **Delivery and expenses.** You log activities, travel and materials expenses, which the cooperative reimburses under its rules.
7. **Invoicing.** The cooperative invoices the client and manages collection.

## Specific points

Costly materials, such as pigments, supports or equipment, must be agreed in advance: the engagement must clarify who buys them and how they are reimbursed. The works you create remain subject to copyright rules, to be defined in writing: the cooperative does not decide on your behalf.

For VAT treatment of invoices to EU or non-EU clients each case is assessed. If you live abroad, tax residence must be checked separately. We promise no net amounts: they depend on your situation.

Practical benefits include professional liability insurance in the package, WhatsApp support to clarify contract doubts, and the members' community, useful for swapping contacts with galleries or clients.

## Transport, customs and safety

If the commission involves shipping works or materials, ask the contract to state who handles packing, transport and insurance of the goods. Customs formalities depend on the country and type of goods: clarify them beforehand, not after the work is done. The same applies to work at height or in public places, where permits and safety measures must be guaranteed by the client.

## Documenting your work

For an artist, keeping sketches, photos of work stages and exchanges with the client is useful in several ways: it shows authorship, serves as portfolio and clarifies any disputes. Ask the client for written approval of drafts before moving to the final execution: it is the simplest protection against late change requests.
