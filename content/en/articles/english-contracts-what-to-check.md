A foreign client often sends its own contract template, drafted in English, running to several pages. It is tempting to skim and sign. Better to stop: clauses that look standard can weigh heavily on the relationship. Here is what to check, with the English terms worth recognising.

## Who the counterparty is

Check the names and details of the parties. If you work through a cooperative, the contracting party is the cooperative, not you. If the document names you as an individual "Contractor" or "Consultant", it must be adapted before proceeding.

## Scope of work and deliverables

The perimeter of the work. Look at how activities, expected results and acceptance criteria are described. Phrases like "and any other tasks as required" widen the scope beyond what was agreed.

## Term and termination

Duration and how to exit. Check the notice period, whether it is mutual, and whether the client can end the contract without notice ("termination for convenience") while you cannot.

## Fees, invoicing and payment terms

Rate, currency, invoicing frequency, payment terms (for example "net 30"), late-payment interest, reimbursable expenses. Check when terms start running: from the invoice date or from its approval?

## Confidentiality and NDA

How long the confidentiality duty lasts, what information it covers, what exceptions apply. Some clauses continue after the relationship ends.

## Intellectual property

Who owns the results ("work made for hire", "assignment of rights")? Check whether it extends to pre-existing material or your personal tools.

## Exclusivity and non-compete

They can prevent you from working with other clients or competitors. Check scope, duration and territory.

## Liability, indemnification and insurance

Liability limits, indemnity obligations, required policies. "Unlimited liability" can be very heavy.

## Governing law and jurisdiction

Which law applies and where disputes are heard. With non-EU clients, the practical ability to enforce your rights can change a lot.

## Example: Irene, mobile developer

A fictional example: Irene receives a twelve-page contract from a US company. She reads it with a checklist: contracting party (an individual is named), intellectual property clause (includes pre-existing code), non-compete (two years, the whole sector), payment at 60 days from approval. She flags the points to the cooperative, which proposes changes: the contract is in the cooperative's name, the IP scope is limited to delivered work, the non-compete is reduced. The client partly accepts and Irene decides with clear data whether to proceed.

## What to check

- Whether specific legal advice is needed, especially for high-value contracts.
- Consistency with your employment contract and the regulation.
- That no clause binds you personally in a way different from the cooperative model.
