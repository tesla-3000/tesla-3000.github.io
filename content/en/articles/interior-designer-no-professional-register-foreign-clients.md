Many interior designers are not registered with the architects' professional body: they run design studios, have showroom experience, specialist training. More and more foreign clients, private and corporate, look for them for interior design: a villa in Portugal, a restaurant in Dubai, a flat in London. The legitimate question is: what can be done without registration and how to organise the relationship? {{< brand >}} works within clear limits.

## What you can and cannot do

Professions with a register and a reserved scope cannot be handled with this structure: building and structural design, signing technical documents, permits with the council, site supervision and certifications are reserved to registered professionals. A designer who is not registered can, however, do design and consulting: **concept** and moodboards, **functional layout** (as long as it is not a technical act), **selection of materials, colours and finishes**, **furniture and lighting**, **renders and visualisations**, **sourcing** of products and suppliers, **purchase coordination**. The exact scope is checked case by case before accepting an assignment. Rules may also vary with the client's country.

## How an assignment for a foreign client is set up

The foreign client signs an agreement with the cooperative for design services. If a technical project or permit is needed, a local professional (an architect or engineer qualified in the country of the site) usually steps in and signs the documents. Pay may be by **phase** (concept, development, sourcing), by **day** or as a **percentage** of furniture purchases, the latter to be weighed carefully. The cooperative invoices; for EU clients normally under reverse charge (art. 7-ter of decree 633/72), for non-EU clients as generic B2B services outside Italian VAT, checked case by case (private clients follow different VAT rules).

## The worker-member's role

As a member (Italian Law 142/2001) you have a work relationship with the cooperative: INPS contributions, INAIL and, if employment, paid leave, sick leave, severance and a payslip. The professional liability insurance included covers activity within the agreed scope.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Confusing design with technical design.** The main risk: do not sign, certify or supervise works.
- **Title.** Do not use the title "architect" if you are not entitled to.
- **Purchases on the client's behalf.** Clarify who orders, who pays and who answers for defects.
- **Rights in projects.** Renders, drawings and layouts have specific copyright rules.
- **Site abroad.** Permits and local rules depend on the country.

## Example

Example: imagine Marta, an interior designer from Verona not on the register, asked by a couple in Lisbon for the interior design of a renovated flat. The agreement covers concept, selection of furniture and finishes, renders and sourcing in Italy. A Portuguese architect signs the local technical documents. Marta logs days on the platform and receives a monthly payslip; the cooperative invoices the client. Fictional character.

## Compared with a VAT number

The flat-rate regime (revenue up to 85,000 euro, 15% substitute tax, 5% for the first five years if conditions are met) is widely used by designers: autonomy and low costs, but contributions and paperwork are yours, and the same limits on reserved activities apply. It does not change what you may do: it changes how you organise it.

## Checklist before signing

Before accepting the assignment, write down what you will and will not do: list the design activities included and those excluded (technical project, permits, site supervision, certifications). Ask the client who the local professional will be for reserved parts and coordinate timing with them. Define an indicative furnishing budget and who decides purchases. Present your title correctly, for example "interior designer" and not "architect". If you doubt the scope, ask us for a check before signing: it is the best protection for you and the client.
