An IT project with developers in Poland, designers in Portugal, the product owner in London and the client in New York: the project manager holds people, deadlines and budget together across time zones. When the PM is an external professional, the client often wants a monthly invoice and a full-time commitment. {{< brand >}} proposes a work structure with protections.

## What a PM actually does in a remote team

The work is concrete: sprint and roadmap planning, agile ceremonies (planning, daily, retrospective), backlog management in Jira or similar, risks and dependencies, sponsor reports, budget control. In a distributed team, communication rules weigh heavily: overlap hours, channels (Slack, email, calls), documentation. The client judges predictability: regular deliveries, reliable estimates, early warning of problems.

Agree in the contract:

- role and responsibilities (what the PM decides, what the client does);
- percentage of commitment and overlap hours;
- tools and access;
- duration and notice.

## The cooperative's role

The cooperative signs the agreement with the client and invoices. For EU clients the reverse charge normally applies (art. 7-ter of decree 633/72); for non-EU clients generic B2B services are normally outside Italian VAT: checked case by case. As a worker-member (Italian Law 142/2001) you have a work relationship with INPS contributions, INAIL and, if employment, paid leave, sick leave, severance and a payslip. A full-time commitment to one client suits an employment relationship well.

## Typical pitfalls

- **De facto subordination.** A PM embedded in a team has hours and directions: an employment relationship is more coherent than false self-employment.
- **Responsibility for outcomes.** Clarify what is an obligation of means and what of result.
- **Budget and scope.** Scope changes without budget review are the classic problem.
- **Responsibility for other people.** Coordinating does not mean disciplinary power.
- **Endless meetings.** Set a cap on hours and cadence.

## Example

Example: imagine Stefano, a project manager in Rome, hired by a fintech scale-up in Dublin to lead a team of eight across Poland, Portugal and Ireland: six months, full time. The agreement fixes 10 to 17 CET overlap, sprint cadence and reporting. The cooperative invoices monthly; Stefano logs attendance and activities on the platform and receives a payslip with employee protections. Fictional character.

## How it works with the cooperative

1. **Intro call.** You explain role, duration, commitment, rate.
2. **Joining.** Share of capital, refundable on exit under the bylaws.
3. **Agreement.** Contract with the client.
4. **Employment contract.** Your relationship with the cooperative.
5. **Activity.** Attendance, reports and expenses on the platform.
6. **Invoicing and payslip.** The cooperative invoices and collects; you receive the payslip.

INAIL, professional liability insurance and WhatsApp/email support are included.

## Compared with a VAT number

The flat-rate regime (revenue up to 85,000 euro, 15% substitute tax, 5% for the first five years if conditions are met) works if you have several clients and margin; for long full-time assignments with a single client the cooperative simplifies admin and offers more protections.

## Checklist before signing

Before accepting, ask for: the org chart and who sponsors the project; the budget and how it is tracked; the methodology in use (Scrum, Kanban, hybrid); backlog status and technical debt; tools and licences. Ask for a week of onboarding with all stakeholders. Define report frequency and the criteria by which your work is judged: deadlines, quality, team satisfaction. In a remote team, a shared communication-rules document from day one avoids a lot of friction.
