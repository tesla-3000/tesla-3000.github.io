It happens often: an Italian developer is noticed by a US startup, the collaboration goes well, and the startup asks for a whole module of its product to be built. The client asks for one simple thing: a vendor that invoices. For someone without a VAT number, this is where things stall. With {{< brand >}} there is another route: working as a worker-member of a cooperative, with an employment contract and a payslip.

## What US clients usually expect

American companies hiring external developers think in terms of "contractors": a vendor that delivers defined work and invoices for it, under a services agreement (an MSA plus a statement of work). They typically ask for:

- a clearly identifiable entity that invoices on time;
- confidentiality and assignment of rights on the code produced;
- some availability windows for calls that fit the US time zone;
- payment in dollars by international transfer.

The cooperative covers all of this: it is the vendor, it signs the commercial agreement and it invoices. For B2B services to non-EU clients, generic services are normally not subject to Italian VAT, though each case is checked individually.

## Employment contract instead of a VAT number

Under Italian Law 142/2001, a worker-member has, besides the membership relationship, a further and distinct work relationship with the cooperative, which can be an employment contract. Social security (INPS) is paid by the cooperative, there is INAIL accident insurance, and paid leave, sick leave, parental leave and severance (TFR) accrue. If you lose your job involuntarily, unemployment benefit (NASpI) may be available under the legal conditions. You get a monthly payslip and an annual tax certificate, with no bookkeeping, no e-invoicing and no tax returns to manage on the business side.

The flat-rate VAT regime remains a valid option, especially with several clients and healthy margins, but you pay your own contributions and handle the paperwork. If you want employee protections without handling the admin, the cooperative is worth comparing.

## Typical pitfalls in software work

- **Intellectual property.** A US client will want assignment of the code. The clause belongs in the commercial agreement, not in a private side deal.
- **NDAs and access.** Repository and environment access should be traceable.
- **Milestones and acceptance.** A price per milestone with clear acceptance criteria works best: payment timing depends on the client's acceptance, so write it down.
- **Time zones.** Agree call windows from day one; you organise the rest of the work.

## Example

Example: imagine Marco, a 32-year-old backend developer in Bologna working for a startup in Austin. The startup asks him to build its payments module (API and integrations) over six months, with monthly milestones and acceptance tests. Marco does not want a VAT number. The cooperative signs the service contract with the startup (scope, milestones, price per milestone, code assignment, confidentiality) and engages Marco as a worker-member. Marco organises the work and records activities and deliveries on the platform; at each accepted milestone the cooperative invoices in dollars, and Marco receives his payslip every month. ## How it works with the cooperative

1. **Intro call.** You describe the project: client, scope, deliverables, price, payment terms.
2. **Joining.** You become a member by subscribing a share of capital, refundable on exit according to the bylaws.
3. **Contract.** The cooperative signs the service contract with the foreign client.
4. **Employment contract.** You receive yours, in line with the cooperative's internal regulation.
5. **Monthly activity.** You record activities and deliveries on the platform; expenses are reimbursed.
6. **Invoice and payslip.** The cooperative invoices, collects (also in foreign currency) and pays you through payroll. A payment advance from a dedicated fund is available if needed.

## What to check first

If you live, or plan to live, abroad, tax residence and double taxation treaties must be assessed case by case; we cannot promise a uniform outcome. If the client asks for exclusivity, fixed hours or to fold you into its team like an employee, we talk first: we do not place people with companies that manage them as their own employees, and in that case the right route is direct hiring.

## Checklist before signing

Before accepting the offer, prepare these points: the text of the MSA or statement of work with its clauses on code ownership and confidentiality; the price per milestone, the acceptance criteria and the currency; the invoicing calendar (usually month end, with payment at 15, 30 or 45 days); call windows with the US team; how out-of-scope requests are handled. If the client proposes a trial period, write down how long it lasts and how it is paid. The clearer these points are from the start, the fewer arguments you will have mid-project.
