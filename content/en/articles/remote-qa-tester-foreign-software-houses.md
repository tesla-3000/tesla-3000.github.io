A QA tester is the last line of defence before release: they open the app or site as a user would, try journeys, edge cases and regressions, and report defects so developers can reproduce them. Foreign software houses often hire remote QA to cover releases, device testing and Italian localisation. {{< brand >}} structures the work with a contract and a payslip.

## What you deliver

Deliverables are concrete: test plan, test cases, bug reports with reproduction steps, screenshots or video, regression reports, localisation checks (Italian text, date formats, currency), accessibility testing. Those who automate write scripts with Cypress, Playwright or Selenium and plug them into the pipeline. Tools are the client's: Jira, TestRail, BrowserStack. Work often comes in peaks: little between releases, a lot in release week.

Agree on:

- scope (web, iOS, Android, API) and devices;
- acceptance criteria and bug severity;
- hours during release phases;
- work metric (hours, test cases run).

## The cooperative's role

The cooperative signs the agreement and invoices. For EU clients the reverse charge normally applies (art. 7-ter of decree 633/72); for non-EU clients generic B2B services are normally outside Italian VAT: checked case by case. As a worker-member (Italian Law 142/2001) you have a work relationship with INPS contributions, INAIL and, if employment, paid leave, sick leave, severance and a payslip. The contract with the client describes the testing service: scope, test cycles, defect reports and delivery times. You organise the work.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Peaky work.** Ask for a monthly minimum commitment to have stable income.
- **Unreproducible bugs.** Vague reports waste time: standardise the format.
- **Devices.** Testing across many devices needs a pool or a cloud service: who pays?
- **Release pressure.** Who decides whether a bug blocks release? Not QA alone.
- **Test data.** Using real customer data is a privacy risk.

## Example

Example: imagine Alessia, a QA tester from Pescara, working for a software house in Tallinn on a payments app. She does manual tests on new releases and checks Italian localisation; two days a week, with three full days in release week. The agreement fixes 12 days a month. Alessia logs activities on the platform and receives a monthly payslip. Fictional character.

## How it works with the cooperative

1. **Intro call.** You present skills, tools, commitment, rate.
2. **Joining.** Share of capital, refundable on exit under the bylaws.
3. **Agreement.** Contract with the software house.
4. **Activity.** Hours, test cases and bugs on the platform.
5. **Invoicing.** The cooperative invoices and collects.
6. **Payslip.** Monthly pay and documents online.

INAIL, professional liability insurance, training and WhatsApp/email support are included.

## Compared with a VAT number

The flat-rate regime (revenue up to 85,000 euro, 15% substitute tax, 5% for the first five years if conditions are met) is an option, but contributions and paperwork are yours; for a continuous role with few clients the cooperative cuts bureaucracy.

## Checklist before signing

Before starting, ask for: requirements documentation and acceptance criteria; access to test environments with dummy data; the device and browser matrix; the bug report template; developers' response times. Propose creating a shared regression test set from the first weeks: it gives your work lasting value. Agree a minimum monthly commitment, because release peaks should not leave you with empty months. Record activities on the platform.
