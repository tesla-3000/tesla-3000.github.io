You have three long-standing Italian clients, an agency in Madrid and a company in Chicago just arrived. Your work is the same, but the management is not: invoices in euro and dollars, different deadlines, clients asking for different documents. In practical terms, having both Italian and foreign clients is a growth opportunity, but it requires **organising by flows**.

## The three flows to distinguish

1. **Italian clients**: e-invoicing, ordinary VAT, payments with timing tied to national practice.
2. **EU clients**: B2B services with specific VAT rules (reverse charge, art. 7-ter of Presidential Decree 633/72) and reporting obligations, to be verified case by case.
3. **Non-EU clients**: generic B2B services not subject to VAT in Italy, with payments often in foreign currency and different timing.

Each flow has different times, currencies and documents. For a freelancer, the risk is that foreign clients, few but valuable, bring more bureaucracy than they earn.

## Possible strategies

- **Everything under a VAT number**: a single structure, maximum autonomy. It requires handling invoices in several currencies and attention to VAT obligations and foreign-related reporting.
- **VAT number for Italy and cooperative for abroad**: foreign clients go through the cooperative and for that part you receive a payslip; for Italian clients you stay as you are. Check cases such as compatibility with the flat-rate regime and revenue limits.
- **Everything in the cooperative**: for those who prefer a single payslip and no bookkeeping, noting that Italian clients then sign the contract with the cooperative.

The choice depends on how much foreign clients weigh and how much admin you want to handle.

## A simple method for keeping accounts in order

Spend an hour a week on personal bookkeeping: record income, deadlines and hours for each client, with currency and expected payment date. Each month calculate how much each client weighs in the total and how much time it takes per euro collected: a prestigious foreign client often proves less profitable than a small but punctual Italian one. Keep a liquidity buffer for delays, particularly with non-EU clients. Review the structure every six months, because the best mix changes when clients change.

## Example: three Italian and two foreign clients

*A fictional example.* Imagine Valeria, a communications consultant with a flat-rate VAT number and three Italian clients. A Spanish agency and a US company arrive: revenue grows, invoices in two currencies, requests for documents. With an accountant she checks that adding foreign clients does not push her over the regime's revenue threshold or complicate her obligations. ## How it works with the cooperative

1. **Map your clients**: country, currency, volume, frequency.
2. **Compatibility check** with your current position (VAT number, regime, other employment relationships).
3. **Membership** with a share of capital refundable on exit (Law 142/2001).
4. **Separate contracts** for foreign clients, with rates and payment terms.
5. **Platform**: hours per contract, expenses, documents; one place to see everything.
6. **Payslip and invoicing**: the cooperative invoices foreign clients and you receive the payslip with INPS and INAIL; professional liability included.

## A check to always make

Before combining two modes, check with a professional: flat-rate regime thresholds, contributions, possible incompatibilities between a VAT number and employment relationships, effects on your tax return. A mixed solution can work well, but must be designed with care.
