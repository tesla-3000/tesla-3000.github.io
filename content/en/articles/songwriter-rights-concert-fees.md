Anyone who plays live has income in pieces: gig fees, lessons, studio sessions, performance royalties, record or streaming sales. Each channel follows its own rules. So before talking about a cooperative, it helps to distinguish what is work from what is a right.

## What can go through {{< brand >}}

**Work performances**: a concert, a set in a venue, an evening at a festival, a studio session, a workshop. These are services with set date, place and fee. In these cases the cooperative can sign the contract with the promoter, engage you under an employment relationship (Law 142/2001) and handle payslips, contributions and invoicing.

What normally does **not** go through the cooperative: copyright and related rights, managed through the channels the law provides, and sales of works or recordings. Here too we check case by case and refer you to whoever deals specifically with those rights.

## Example: Davide and the German tour

Example: imagine Davide, a songwriter, who gets an offer from a small German festival for five evenings. He also teaches guitar and does not want a VAT number just for these shows. He joins the cooperative.

The cooperative signs a commercial contract with the festival covering dates, fee and logistics. Davide logs rehearsal and performance days on the platform, claims train and accommodation under the rules, and receives a payslip at month end. For his songs, he continues to manage rights the way he already knows.

## How it works with the cooperative

1. **Booking details.** You send dates, place, promoter and proposed fee.
2. **Check.** We verify whether the booking can be handled as paid work in the cooperative and which rules apply in the foreign country.
3. **Membership.** You become a member with the share capital quota.
4. **Contract with the promoter.** The cooperative formalises it, including fee, times and technical conditions.
5. **Employment relationship.** We define your engagement for the booking period.
6. **Performance and expenses.** You log activities and costs; the cooperative reimburses under its regulation.
7. **Collection.** We invoice and collect, including in foreign currency.

## Things to know

Concerts in other countries raise particular issues: taxation of the fee, possible local withholding, documents for working abroad. We cannot give general answers: we assess them on the individual booking, including double taxation treaties where relevant.

Songwriters often have several activities: gig fees, teaching, production. It helps to keep them distinct so that what goes through the cooperative and what stays outside is not confused. On contributions, employee work in the cooperative involves INPS contributions and INAIL coverage; holidays, sick leave and TFR follow the rules of the relationship.

One practical aspect: bookings are often irregular. The cooperative lets you remain a member between gigs, without closing and reopening positions. We promise neither continuous work nor amounts: they depend on the bookings you find.

## Fees, expenses and payment timing

In contracts with foreign promoters it helps to specify what the fee includes: travel, accommodation, meals, technicians, instruments. Clarify the payment deadline too: some promoters pay after the show, others at the end of the tour. The cooperative can consider an advance through the fund, useful when travel costs precede collection.

## Equipment, merchandise and sales on site

Many musicians sell records or T-shirts after the show. These sales are a different activity from the artistic performance and have their own tax rules: they are not part of the work relationship with the cooperative and must be handled separately. If you travel with valuable instruments, also ask what the insurance covers and whether a dedicated transport policy is needed.
