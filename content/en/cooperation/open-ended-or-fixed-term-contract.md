When you join a worker cooperative as a worker-member, you sign an employment contract. One of the first choices is its duration: **open-ended** (*tempo indeterminato*) or **fixed-term** (*tempo determinato*)? There is no one-size-fits-all answer: it depends on how your work is structured, how steady your clients are and your plans. This guide sets out the general rules and the criteria for choosing.

## The two forms in brief

**Open-ended:** the contract has no end date. It stays in force until one party ends it under the rules (resignation, dismissal in the cases allowed, withdrawal or exclusion as a member with the related effects). It is the standard form of employment in Italy.

**Fixed-term:** the contract has an end date or is tied to a defined period. At expiry it ends automatically, unless extended or renewed.

Either way the member gets a payslip, INPS contributions, INAIL insurance, holidays, sick leave, maternity and paternity leave, and severance pay (TFR).

## General rules of fixed-term contracts

Italian law governs fixed-term work with a few core rules, which have been changed several times over the years:

- there is a **maximum total duration** for fixed-term relationships with the same employer;
- beyond an initial period, extensions and renewals require a **justification** (*causale*) provided by law or collective agreements;
- there is a **maximum number of extensions**;
- **minimum gaps** may be required between one contract and the next;
- collective agreements can adjust certain aspects.

Precisely because these rules change, we give no figures: they must be checked when signing, case by case, against the law in force and the applicable collective agreement.

## When fixed-term may make sense

- **A project with a clear start and end:** for example a few months of consulting for a foreign client. See also [a short three-month project](/en/articles/short-project-three-month-engagement/).
- **A first test:** you want to try the model before committing long term.
- **Seasonal or event-based work.**

## When open-ended may make sense

- **A steady client** or a portfolio of clients that provides continuity.
- **Several clients in parallel** that alternate over time.
- **Life plans** where a contract without an end date matters (for example rentals or loans, where it is often assessed differently).

## What happens between projects

This is the key question for anyone working project by project. The cooperative does not create work out of nothing: pay is funded by the client contracts the member brings in. That is why the internal regulation and the individual contract describe what happens during periods without work.

In general terms:

- with a **fixed-term contract** tied to the project, employment ends at expiry; the person can remain a member and, with a new client contract, sign a new employment contract within the legal limits. In case of involuntary loss of work, under legal conditions, [NASpI unemployment benefit](/en/articles/naspi-protection-when-work-ends/) may apply;
- with an **open-ended contract**, periods without client work are handled under the regulation and individual agreements (for example with holidays, agreed reduced hours or other solutions). These arrangements should be clarified before signing.

## Fictional example

Fictional example: imagine Paolo, a data engineer. A Dutch company offers him six months of work on a data migration. He does not know if there will be a follow-up. With the cooperative he chooses a fixed-term contract aligned with the client contract. In month five the client renews for a year: an extension within legal limits is considered. After eighteen months Paolo has three steady clients and moves to an open-ended contract.
