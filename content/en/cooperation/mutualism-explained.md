"Mutualism" is a word you meet early when you approach a cooperative, yet few people stop to explain it. It is the key to understanding why a worker cooperative works differently from an ordinary company: it does not exist to reward capital, but to give its members something they need. In the case of {{< brand >}}, that something is work under your own employment contract, with the protections that come with it.

## An old idea, born from need

Mutualism took shape in the nineteenth century, when workers, craftspeople and farmers began joining forces to solve problems they could not face alone: buying goods at fair prices, getting credit, securing help during illness, finding work on decent terms. Mutual aid societies and the first cooperatives rested on a simple principle: people with the same need pool resources and effort, and the organisation they create works for them.

In Italy the principle made it into the Constitution: article 45 recognises the social function of cooperation based on mutuality and without private speculative aims. So it is not a folkloric detail, but something the law protects and regulates.

## What it means today, in plain words

Mutualism is an exchange. On one side is the cooperative, on the other its members. The cooperative offers members a benefit linked to its activity; members, in return, take part in the business: they subscribe a share, respect the by-laws and internal regulation, and contribute their work.

Depending on the type of cooperative, the exchange takes different forms:

- in consumer cooperatives the member buys goods on favourable terms;
- in user cooperatives the member uses a service;
- in **worker cooperatives** the member gets the opportunity to work, with a contract and its protections.

For people with foreign clients or who work independently, the mutual exchange is exactly this: the cooperative provides a structure (contracts, invoicing, payslips, insurance, support) that lets the member work as an employee with their own contract instead of on their own.

## Prevailing mutuality: articles 2512 and 2513 of the Civil Code

The Italian Civil Code distinguishes cooperatives with **prevailing mutuality** (mutualità prevalente) from the others. Under article 2512, a cooperative has prevailing mutuality when it operates mainly in favour of its members or, for worker cooperatives, relies mainly on the work of its members.

Article 2513 explains how this is measured: directors and statutory auditors document it in the notes to the financial statements. For worker cooperatives the parameter compares the cost of members' work with the total cost of labour; prevalence exists when the former exceeds half of the latter. The calculation follows technical rules, which is why it is checked on the accounts, year by year.

The by-laws of prevailing-mutuality cooperatives also contain certain clauses required by article 2514, limiting the distribution of profits and reserves: the cooperative's assets are not there to enrich investors, but to sustain the mutual activity over time.

## What it means for the worker-member

For someone joining a worker cooperative, mutualism has a few concrete consequences:

- **work comes first**: the cooperative exists to provide work to its members, not to hold back margins for outside investors;
- **you are part of the business**: besides the working relationship, the member has a membership relationship, with rights to information and participation (see [worker-member rights and duties](/en/cooperation/worker-member-rights-and-duties/));
- **any surplus returns to members**: if the year allows it, the members' meeting can resolve to pay patronage refunds in proportion to the mutual exchange (article 2545-sexies), with no guaranteed amount; see [how patronage refunds work](/en/articles/patronage-refunds-cooperative/);
- **the rules are written down**: by-laws and internal regulation set out how the exchange works in practice.

Mutualism is not a promise of earnings. It is a way of organising work in which the people who work are inside the business, not outside it.

## Fictional example: Irene, translator

Fictional example: imagine Irene, a translator working for two foreign agencies. On her own she would need a VAT number, invoices and paperwork. By joining the cooperative she becomes a member: she subscribes the share and accepts the by-laws and regulation. Then she signs her employment contract. The cooperative signs the commercial contracts with the agencies, invoices and collects; Irene receives her payslip. For her the mutual exchange is a shared organisation that lets her work with an employee's protections. At year end she reads the financial statements and their notes, where she also finds the information on prevalence.

## How it works with the cooperative

1. **Learn the rules.** Before joining, read the by-laws and the [internal regulation](/en/articles/cooperative-internal-regulation/).
2. **Become a member.** Application and subscription of the [share capital](/en/articles/share-capital-what-it-is/).
3. **Employment contract.** The second relationship, distinct from membership.
4. **Work.** {{< brand >}} handles the client contract, invoicing, collection and payroll; the member reports through the digital platform.
5. **Take part.** The member receives information on the cooperative's life and attends the members' meeting.

## Limits worth knowing

Mutualism does not replace the market: without client engagements there is no work to exchange. It does not remove tax and social security rules, which remain those of employment. And prevailing-mutuality status is not automatic: it depends on each year's figures, which must be checked case by case.
