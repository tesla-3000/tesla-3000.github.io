When you join a worker cooperative you rightly focus on your contract and your payslip. But another question is worth asking: who decides, who manages and who checks? A cooperative's governance has its own rules, designed for a business owned by the people who work in it. This page describes the main bodies and the checks in general terms: each cooperative's details are in its by-laws.

## A three-layer structure

Simplifying, a cooperative's governance rests on three layers:

1. **who sets the direction:** the members' meeting;
2. **who manages:** the management body;
3. **who checks:** the internal supervisory body where required, and external checks, starting with the cooperative audit.

On top of these comes a cross-cutting element: transparency towards members and the outside world.

## The members' meeting

This body brings together all members. It approves the financial statements, appoints the directors and, where required, the supervisory body, approves the internal regulation and decides on any patronage refunds. It meets in ordinary or extraordinary form depending on the matter. For details, see [how the members' meeting works](/en/cooperation/members-meeting-how-it-works/).

## The management body

Running the business is the job of the directors, appointed by the members' meeting (article 2542 of the Italian Civil Code). It is usually a board of directors; the composition of the body depends on the Civil Code rules that apply to the specific cooperative and on its by-laws, and should be checked case by case. The Civil Code requires most directors to be chosen from among the members.

What directors do in practice:

- they run the business: client contracts, work organisation, relations with banks and suppliers;
- they decide on admitting new members, according to the criteria in the by-laws (article 2528);
- they prepare the financial statements and management report, which also explains the criteria followed to pursue the mutual purpose (article 2545);
- they call the members' meeting and carry out its decisions.

The board may delegate some functions to one or more directors, within legal limits. Some decisions cannot be delegated, including those on admitting, withdrawing and expelling members and those affecting mutual relationships with members (article 2544).

## The supervisory body and the auditor

The board of statutory auditors (collegio sindacale), or a single auditor where allowed, oversees compliance with law and by-laws and the adequacy of the organisation. In cooperatives it is not always mandatory: article 2543 requires it in certain cases, linked for example to the cooperative's size or to the issue of particular financial instruments. The by-laws may provide for it anyway.

The statutory audit of accounts, which checks the financial statements are correct, may be entrusted to the supervisory body or to an external auditor, under the applicable rules. Again, whether and how these are in place depends on the specific case.

## The cooperative audit

A distinctive feature of cooperatives is the **cooperative audit** (revisione cooperativa): a periodic external check required by law, carried out by the supervisory authority or by the recognised national representative associations the cooperative belongs to. It is not limited to the accounts: it checks that the cooperative is genuinely mutual in nature, that members take part in its life, that management is sound and that mutuality rules are respected.

For worker-members it is an extra safeguard: someone outside the cooperative periodically checks that the mutual pact is not just on paper. The audit report is a document worth asking about.

## Transparency: what is public and what members can see

- **Business Register:** by-laws, director appointments and financial statements are filed and publicly available.
- **Register of Cooperatives:** every cooperative is entered in the Albo delle società cooperative (article 2512), which also shows whether it has prevailing mutuality.
- **Member rights:** besides the financial statements, members can inspect the members' register and the book of members' meetings and resolutions (article 2545-bis), in the ways provided.

For more on mutuality see [mutualism explained](/en/cooperation/mutualism-explained/), and on member rights see [worker-member rights and duties](/en/cooperation/worker-member-rights-and-duties/).

## Fictional example: Marco, data analyst

Fictional example: imagine Marco, a data analyst with a Swiss client, who is considering joining a cooperative. Before signing he wants to understand its governance. He looks the cooperative up in the Register of Cooperatives and finds its prevailing-mutuality status. He asks for the by-laws and learns how the board of directors is composed and how the members' meeting is called. He asks whether there is a supervisory body and when the last cooperative audit took place. He gets clear answers and goes ahead knowing who manages and who checks the business he is about to become part of.
