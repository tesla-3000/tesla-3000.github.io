When people think of cooperatives, they often picture small, local outfits. Most cooperatives are indeed modest in size, but the cooperative model has shown it can grow into some of the largest businesses in their countries. This page gathers a few examples, not to compare, but to show what people can build when they bring work and enterprise together.

Figures are rounded and taken from official documents, listed at the bottom of the page. They change every year: read them as orders of magnitude.

## Mondragon, Basque Country (Spain)

Born in the 1950s in a small Basque town around a technical school, Mondragon is today a group of industrial, retail, financial and education cooperatives. According to the group's 2024 annual report, **around 70,000 people** work there, with aggregate sales of **over 11 billion euros**.

The lesson: a network of cooperatives can share common services, such as training, research and financial tools, while each keeps its own autonomy. The group even has its own university, grown from the original technical school.

## John Lewis Partnership, United Kingdom

The group behind John Lewis department stores and Waitrose supermarkets is technically not a cooperative: it is an **employee-owned business**, held in trust for the benefit of the people who work there. Workers are called "Partners" and share in profits under the group's rules. According to the group's official statement published in 2026, there are **over 65,000 Partners**, and it describes itself as the UK's largest employee-owned business.

The lesson: the idea that those who work are also, collectively, the owners can work at scale and for a century, even outside the legal form of a cooperative.

## Smart, Belgium

Smart is a Belgian cooperative created to give artists, creatives and independent workers a shared structure: members carry out their own activity while the cooperative handles contracts, invoicing and their status as workers. According to the cooperative's 2023 general meeting documents, at the end of 2022 it had **over 34,000 members**.

The lesson: a cooperative can be the shared enterprise of tens of thousands of independent professionals, who pool the administrative side to focus on their craft.

## Coopaname, France

France has a specific form, the **coopérative d'activité et d'emploi (CAE)**: people developing their own activity become "entrepreneur-salariés", working under a contract with the cooperative, and can later become members. Coopaname, founded in Paris in the early 2000s, is one of the largest and best-known CAEs, with professionals from very different trades. Overall, according to ESS France (2025), French CAEs support **around 12,000 entrepreneurs**.

The lesson: self-employed work can be organised collectively and mutually, with an employment contract and shared services, even in a legal system that recognises it specifically.

## Italy's large cooperatives

Italy has one of the richest cooperative traditions in Europe, across many sectors: retail, agriculture, services, construction, credit, catering, social care.

The most visible example is the **Coop system** of consumer cooperatives: according to 2024 figures released by Coop Italia, it has **around 6.2 million members**. The largest cooperative in the system, **Coop Alleanza 3.0**, has **around 2.2 million members** according to its own reports.

Alongside consumer cooperatives there are large worker cooperatives in services, contract catering, logistics and construction, each with thousands of worker-members, and social cooperatives, which in many areas are among the main employers.

The lesson: in Italy cooperation is deeply rooted and recognised by the Constitution (Article 45), which acknowledges its social function.

## What they have in common

Sectors and sizes vary widely, but some traits recur:

- **people first:** the business exists to serve its members, whether workers or consumers;
- **mutuality:** the benefits of the shared enterprise go back to those who belong to it, including through a share of surpluses;
- **long term:** many of these organisations have decades of history and have weathered major economic crises;
- **shared services:** training, administrative tools and networks no individual could afford alone;
- **participation:** meetings, information for members and clear internal rules.
