Cooperatives around the world share seven principles, set out by the International Cooperative Alliance and restated in the 1995 Statement on the Cooperative Identity. Put like that, it sounds like textbook material. Yet for a freelancer considering a worker cooperative, these principles make a very practical checklist: they tell you what to expect and what to ask.

Let us go through them one by one, from the point of view of someone who works with clients, often abroad, and wants an employment contract instead of an Italian VAT number.

## 1. Voluntary and open membership

A cooperative is open to anyone who can use its services and accepts the responsibilities of membership, without discrimination. For a freelancer this means two things: you join by choice, not because a client imposes it, and admission criteria are written in the articles and applied equally to all. Likewise, you can leave, under the withdrawal rules.

## 2. Democratic member control

The cooperative is controlled by its members, not by an outside investor. In practice, control runs through the **members' meeting**, which approves the accounts and appoints the governing bodies, and through a board of directors accountable to that meeting. For a freelancer, what matters most is **transparency**: receiving clear information on accounts, rules and decisions; being able to attend meetings, ask questions and suggest improvements. You are not a "customer" of a service: you are part of the organisation.

## 3. Member economic participation

Members contribute to the cooperative's capital, usually through a membership share, and control how it is used. Capital receives limited return, if any. Any surplus is used to develop the cooperative (reserves, largely indivisible), to return benefits to members in proportion to their dealings (patronage refunds) and to support other activities approved by members. We cover this in [cooperative surplus, refunds and reserves](/en/cooperation/surplus-patronage-refunds-reserves/).

## 4. Autonomy and independence

A cooperative is an autonomous organisation controlled by its members. If it enters agreements with others or raises outside capital, it does so while keeping member control. For someone working with a foreign client this is reassuring: the cooperative does not depend on a single client, and the rules of the employment relationship are set in the articles and internal regulation, not dictated by the client.

## 5. Education, training and information

Cooperatives provide training for members, directors and staff, and inform the public about cooperation. For a freelancer this means training opportunities, clear material on their rights (payslip, contributions, severance pay) and a community to compare notes on contracts, rates and clients.

## 6. Cooperation among cooperatives

Cooperatives grow stronger by working together locally, nationally and internationally. In Italy one concrete form is the share of profits paid to mutual funds that promote cooperation. For members it also opens doors: networks, skill exchanges, joint projects.

## 7. Concern for community

Cooperatives work for the sustainable development of their communities through policies approved by members. For a worker cooperative this means, for example, offering regular, protected work to people who would otherwise have no safety net, and letting professionals work from Italy with clients anywhere in the world.

## Fictional example

Fictional example: imagine Davide, a developer working for a Canadian startup. Before joining a cooperative he turns the seven principles into questions: can I join and leave under clear rules? (1) Who controls the cooperative and how am I kept informed? (2) What happens to any surplus? (3) Does my contract depend on the client? (4) Is there training? (5) Is the cooperative part of a network? (6) What impact does it have? (7). He reads the articles and the regulation, attends an information session, then decides to join.

## How it works with the cooperative

1. **Know the rules.** {{< brand >}} shares its articles and internal regulation before admission.
2. **Admission.** Application, subscription of the share, acceptance of the rules.
3. **Employment contract.** The member is hired under a contract linked to the regulation.
4. **Participation.** Regular information, the members' meeting, support channels and community.
5. **Shared results.** Any surplus follows the allocations set by law and the articles.

## Why they matter

The principles are not slogans: they help tell a genuine cooperative from an organisation that only borrows the legal form.
