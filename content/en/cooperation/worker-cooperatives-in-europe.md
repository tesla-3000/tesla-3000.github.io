Working independently, choosing your own clients and projects, while holding an employment contract with its protections is not an Italian invention. In several European countries, models have emerged to solve the same problem: how to give social protection to freelancers without forcing them to become one-person businesses.

This page describes the families of models in general terms, without reference to any specific organisation, and explains what the Italian worker-member model shares with them. Each country's rules change over time: treat this information as indicative.

## The common problem

Independent work is growing across Europe: consultants, creatives, developers, trainers. Many have a real but small business and find the paperwork of self-employment heavy and the protections thin: sickness, parenthood, unemployment, pension. All the models below start from here, with different answers.

## France: activity and employment cooperatives

France has had activity and employment cooperatives (*coopératives d'activité et d'emploi*) for decades. A 2014 law on the social and solidarity economy gave recognition to a specific figure: the **entrepreneur-salarié**, the employee-entrepreneur.

In short:

- the person develops their own business with their own clients, independently;
- the cooperative employs them under an employment contract and handles invoicing, accounting and contributions;
- after a period, the person is expected to become a member;
- the cooperative offers support, training and a peer community.

There is often an initial phase for testing the activity before actual employment.

## France: portage salarial (a different model)

France also has **portage salarial**, which should be kept separate. It is a three-way relationship: the professional finds the client and negotiates the work, a portage company employs them and invoices the client, and the client pays the company. The professional has employee status with its protections.

The main difference: a portage company is normally a commercial company, not a cooperative. The professional does not become a member and does not take part in its life. The model is governed by specific employment rules with its own requirements (for example on skill and pay levels).

## Belgium and elsewhere: shared-employment cooperatives

In Belgium and other countries, cooperatives have emerged that let self-employed workers, artists and professionals be employed for individual assignments or periods of activity, pooling administration, insurance and sometimes payment guarantee tools. Many grew out of the arts and entertainment world and later opened to other trades.

Across Europe there are also more traditional worker cooperatives, where members jointly run a common business, and hybrid forms combining individual activities with shared services.

## The Italian model: the worker-member

In Italy the reference is Law 142/2001. A member of a worker cooperative, besides the membership relationship, enters into a "further and distinct working relationship", which may be employment. The cooperative has a filed internal regulation describing how work is organised. For details see [Law 142/2001 explained simply](/en/articles/law-142-2001-explained/).

### What it shares with the other models

- **Independence in work:** the member brings their own clients and in practice negotiates their own rates.
- **Employee contract:** payslip, contributions, protection for sickness, holidays, parenthood and, under legal conditions, unemployment.
- **Shared services:** commercial contract with the client, invoicing, collection, administration.
- **Community and training:** peers to compare notes with.

### What sets it apart

- You are a **member from the start**, with a capital share and acceptance of the articles and regulation.
- **Mutuality** is central: any surplus follows Civil Code rules on patronage refunds and reserves.
- Italian employment law and the Italian social security system apply.

## Fictional example

Fictional example: imagine Lucia, an Italian trainer who spent some years in Lyon working as an employee-entrepreneur in an activity cooperative. Back in Italy, she looks for something similar and discovers the worker-member model: she finds the same independence and protections, under Italian rules and as a member from day one. For pension questions linked to her years in France she asks for a specific check.

## Keep in mind

Each model is tied to its home country. People living and working in Italy follow Italian rules; those moving between countries must check residence, contributions and tax case by case. See also [what worker-member means](/en/articles/what-worker-member-means/).
