In an ordinary company, the people who work there rarely see the shareholders' meeting up close: it belongs to the investors. In a worker cooperative, the members are also the people who work. The members' meeting therefore becomes the place where those who work take part in the business's fundamental decisions. This page explains what it does, how it is called, the difference between ordinary and extraordinary meetings and how decisions are recorded.

## What the members' meeting is

The members' meeting (assemblea dei soci) brings together all members. Cooperatives follow, as far as compatible, the Italian Civil Code rules for joint-stock companies or, where the by-laws so provide and the conditions are met, those for limited liability companies (article 2519), along with the specific rules for cooperatives. Each cooperative's by-laws then set out how it works in practice.

For the worker-member, taking part in the meeting is part of the membership relationship: Law 142/2001 (art. 1, para. 2) recalls that members contribute to managing the business and take part in forming its bodies.

## What the members' meeting does

### Approves the financial statements

This is the main task of the ordinary meeting, at least once a year. The financial statements show how the year went; the notes also contain information on the mutual exchange and on prevalence (article 2513). For members it is the chance to understand the cooperative's health and ask questions. See also [mutualism explained](/en/cooperation/mutualism-explained/).

### Appoints the governing bodies

The meeting appoints the directors and, where required, the supervisory body or auditor. It also sets their term, within the limits of law and by-laws. The full picture is in [cooperative governance](/en/cooperation/cooperative-governance-bodies/).

### Approves the internal regulation

Law 142/2001 (art. 6) gives the members' meeting the task of approving the internal regulation that governs members' working relationships, and any changes to it. To read it methodically, see [reading the internal regulation](/en/cooperation/reading-the-internal-regulation/).

### Decides on patronage refunds

When approving the financial statements, if there is a surplus, the meeting may resolve to pay patronage refunds to members in proportion to the mutual exchange (article 2545-sexies and art. 3 of Law 142/2001). They are never automatic: they depend on results and on the meeting's decision. More in [how patronage refunds work](/en/articles/patronage-refunds-cooperative/).

## Ordinary and extraordinary meetings

- **Ordinary:** handles the regular running of the cooperative's life: financial statements, appointments, remuneration of the bodies, regulation, refunds and other matters the by-laws assign to it.
- **Extraordinary:** deals with the fundamental rules: amendments to the by-laws, extraordinary transactions such as mergers, dissolution and winding-up. The minutes of an extraordinary meeting are drawn up by a notary.

The distinction is not about how important a topic is to an individual member, but about the type of decision: extraordinary matters touch the deed of incorporation and by-laws, so they require more formalities.

## Calling the meeting

The meeting is called by the directors as set out in the by-laws. The notice usually states:

- date, time and place (or how to join remotely);
- the agenda, i.e. the list of items to be discussed;
- often a second date, in case the first meeting does not reach the minimum attendance required.

The by-laws also set the notice period and the means of communication, such as registered mail, certified email (PEC) or other tools that provide proof of receipt. With the notice for the financial statements, the documents are made available to members before the meeting, within the legal time limits.

A member may be represented by another member through a proxy, within the limits set by the Civil Code for cooperatives (article 2539) and by the by-laws.

## The minutes

Every meeting ends with minutes recording who attended, what was discussed and what was decided. For an ordinary meeting they are signed by the chair and the secretary; for an extraordinary one they are drawn up by a notary. Minutes are entered in the book of members' meetings and resolutions, which members are entitled to inspect (article 2545-bis).

## Fictional example: Chiara, UX researcher

Fictional example: imagine Chiara, a UX researcher with a Dutch client, who has been a member for a few months. She receives the notice of the ordinary meeting by certified email: the agenda covers the financial statements, a change to the regulation on expense claims and the appointment of the board of directors. She downloads the documents from the platform, reads the notes and prepares a question on the expense criteria. On the day she joins remotely, listens to the directors' report and asks her question, which goes into the minutes with the answer. A few weeks later she finds the minutes in the documents area.

## How it works with the cooperative

1. **Notice.** {{< brand >}} sends the notice with the agenda as set out in the by-laws.
2. **Documents.** Financial statements, reports and proposals are available on the member's digital platform.
3. **Questions in advance.** Members can ask for clarification by email or WhatsApp before the meeting.
4. **Meeting.** In person or remotely, if the by-laws allow it.
5. **Minutes.** Decisions are recorded and accessible.

## What to check in the by-laws

- Notice periods and methods.
- Whether remote attendance is possible.
- Rules on proxies.
- Matters reserved to the members' meeting.
