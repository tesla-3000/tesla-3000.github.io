Not every freelancer works eight hours a day for one client. Some support a foreign client three days a week, some have peaks in certain months, some combine it with another activity. For these situations a worker-member of a cooperative can have a **part-time** employment contract. This guide explains the possible forms and their effects in general terms.

## The three forms of part-time

### Horizontal

You work every scheduled day, but with reduced hours. Example: four hours a day, Monday to Friday. It suits steady but limited work, such as ongoing support for a client.

### Vertical

You work full time, but only on certain days of the week, certain weeks of the month or certain months of the year. Example: three full days a week, or full time from March to June. It suits project work with a predictable calendar or seasonal work.

### Mixed

It combines both: for example reduced daily hours in some periods and full time in others. It is the most flexible option, useful when the workload follows a known but irregular rhythm.

## The basic rules

A part-time contract must be in writing and state precisely the amount of work and when it is performed (days, weeks, months). The law and collective agreements also provide tools for varying hours, such as flexibility clauses and additional work, with limits and safeguards for the worker. You can switch from part-time to full time and back by written agreement.

For worker-members, the cooperative's internal regulation and applicable collective agreement also apply: these documents set out the details.

## Fitting variable workloads

Part-time is a useful tool when client work does not cover a full-time job. Some criteria:

- **Align hours with the client contract:** contract hours should match those the client actually needs.
- **Plan for growth:** if the client may increase the workload, it helps to know in advance how hours will be raised.
- **Handle peaks:** vertical or mixed part-time lets you concentrate work in the busiest periods.
- **Report regularly:** logging activity and attendance on the platform keeps the contract consistent with actual work.

More on working time in [hours and autonomy in remote work](/en/articles/hours-autonomy-remote-work/).

## Effects on protections and contributions

A part-time worker has the same rights as a full-time one, but many are **proportional** to hours and pay:

- **pay and severance pay (TFR)** are calculated on the contracted hours;
- **holidays** and leave are pro-rated as set by the collective agreement;
- **sick leave, maternity and paternity** are guaranteed, with amounts linked to pay;
- **INPS contributions** are paid on actual pay: fewer hours mean a lower credited amount. There may be effects on pension entitlement and amount, and on the weeks that count, which should be assessed;
- **NASpI**, in case of involuntary loss of work, depends on legal requirements and accumulated contributions.

There are no figures valid for everyone: actual effects must be checked case by case. See also [pension contributions when working through a cooperative](/en/articles/pension-contributions-working-through-cooperative/).

## Part-time and other activities

A part-time worker can generally carry out other activities, respecting duties of loyalty and confidentiality and the terms of the contract and regulation. If the other activity is with another employer or under a VAT number, check compatibility and formalities before starting.

## Fictional example

Fictional example: imagine Chiara, a copywriter who works for a German publisher about three days a week, plus an intensive campaign every autumn. With the cooperative she chooses mixed part-time: three days a week for most of the year, full time during the campaign months. When the client also gives her the monthly newsletter, she signs an agreement to increase her hours.
