People often say cooperatives are "non-profit". That is not quite right: a cooperative is a business and must balance its books, and it may close the year in surplus. The difference lies in **what happens to that surplus**. This guide explains where an operating surplus comes from and where it can go under Italian law: patronage refunds, reserves, the legal reserve, mutual funds and, within limits, return on capital.

For the practical side of refunds to members, see our dedicated article: [patronage refunds in a cooperative](/en/articles/patronage-refunds-cooperative/). Here we look at the whole picture.

## Where a surplus comes from

In a worker cooperative, revenue comes from contracts with clients. Out of that revenue the cooperative pays the salaries and social contributions of worker-members, insurance, shared services (platform, administration, support), taxes and other costs. If income exceeds costs at year end, there is a surplus.

A surplus is not the goal in itself: the cooperative exists to give members a mutual advantage, meaning better working conditions and services than they would get on their own. Still, a surplus is a sign of sound management and lets the cooperative grow stronger over time.

## Patronage refund: the mutual advantage returned to members

The Italian refund, called *ristorno*, is governed by art. 2545-sexies of the Civil Code. It lets the cooperative return to members part of the surplus generated by mutual dealings, in proportion to their quantity and quality. In a worker cooperative the dealing is the work itself, so the refund can be linked to the work each member has done.

Key points:

- the founding deed must set the criteria for distributing refunds;
- the decision belongs to the members' meeting, which approves the financial statements;
- the refund can take forms allowed by law, for example as a pay supplement or as an increase in the member's share;
- it is optional: no surplus or no resolution, no refund.

## Dividend: the return on capital

A dividend is something else: it rewards capital subscribed by the member, not their work. In cooperatives with prevalent mutuality, the Civil Code (art. 2514) requires the articles to limit dividend distribution and to forbid distributing reserves among members. In short, a cooperative is not designed to make capital yield: the member's share is there to take part, not to speculate.

## Indivisible reserves

A large part of the surplus goes to **reserves**. In cooperatives with prevalent mutuality reserves are indivisible: they cannot be shared out among members, either during the cooperative's life or when a member leaves. They are the common assets that provide stability, allow investment, help through difficult periods and fund services (such as tools to advance payments).

This is the heart of the model: newcomers find assets built by those who came before, and those who leave pass their contribution on to those who come next. It is an intergenerational logic.

## The legal reserve

Art. 2545-quater of the Civil Code states that, whatever the size of the legal reserve, at least 30% of annual net profits must be allocated to it. This rule secures the cooperative before any other allocation.

## The share for mutual funds

The same article requires a share of annual net profits, equal to 3%, to be paid to the mutual funds for the promotion and development of cooperation. These funds support the creation and growth of other cooperatives. It is a concrete application of the principle of cooperation among cooperatives: part of each one's result helps the whole movement grow.

## The order of allocations, in brief

Simplifying, when there is a surplus:

1. the cooperative considers refunds to members under the founding deed's criteria (refunds are worked out before determining the profit to allocate);
2. part of the net profit must go to the legal reserve;
3. a share goes to the mutual funds;
4. the rest can go to indivisible reserves or, within the limits of articles and law, to other uses.

Details depend on the articles, the regulation and the members' meeting resolutions: for a specific case, always check the cooperative's documents.

## Fictional example

Fictional example: imagine Irene, a translator and worker-member for three years. One year the cooperative closes with a surplus. The board of directors prepares the financial statements; the members' meeting approves them and, under the agreed criteria, resolves a refund linked to work done. Irene receives it as a supplement on her payslip. Part of the surplus goes to the legal reserve, a share to the mutual funds, the rest to indivisible reserves. The following year the accounts break even: no refund, and Irene knew that could happen, because it had never been promised.

## How it works with the cooperative

1. **Work and contracts.** The member works for clients through {{< brand >}}, which invoices and collects.
2. **Annual accounts.** The board of directors prepares the financial statements, including the note on mutual dealings.
3. **Members' meeting.** Members receive the information and the meeting approves the accounts and the allocation of any surplus.
4. **Possible refund.** If resolved, it is paid under the agreed criteria.
5. **Reserves.** The rest strengthens the common assets.

## Key takeaways

- A surplus is not guaranteed, nor is a refund.
- Refund (work) and dividend (capital) are distinct.
- Indivisible reserves stay with the cooperative.
- The legal reserve and mutual funds have shares set by the Civil Code.
