If you have already read our [Law 142/2001 explained simply](/en/articles/law-142-2001-explained/), you know the core idea: a worker-member has two relationships with the cooperative. This page goes one step further and follows the structure of the law, article by article, so you know where to find what. It is not a full legal commentary: it is a map to help you read your contract and the regulation. Concrete cases always need a specific check.

## Article 1: who the worker-member is and the dual relationship

The first article sets the scope: the law applies to cooperatives in which the mutual relationship concerns work performed by members.

Paragraph 2 describes the role of worker-members: they contribute to managing the business, take part in forming the cooperative's bodies and defining its management structure, take part in drawing up development plans, contribute to share capital and share in business risk, economic results and decisions on how they are used, and make their professional skills available.

Paragraph 3 introduces the central concept: on joining or later, the member enters into a "further and distinct working relationship", as an employee, self-employed or in any other form. This is where the dual relationship comes from.

## Article 2: the worker's rights

Article 2 applies labour protection rules to worker-members in an employment relationship, with some specific features linked to also being members. In practice, for someone with an employment contract, the protections are those of employees; the specific exceptions should be read in the text and, if relevant, assessed with a professional.

## Article 3: pay and patronage refunds

This is the article that matters most to anyone reading their payslip. Paragraph 1 says the cooperative must pay a member in an employment relationship overall pay proportionate to the quantity and quality of work, and in any case not below the minimums of the collective agreements for the sector or a similar category.

Paragraph 2 allows additional payments resolved by the members' meeting: a pay supplement in the ways set by collective agreements or, when the financial statements are approved, patronage refunds of up to 30% of total pay. This is a ceiling set by law, not a forecast of what anyone will receive: refunds exist only if there is a surplus and the members' meeting resolves to pay them. More in [how patronage refunds work](/en/articles/patronage-refunds-cooperative/).

## Article 4: social security

Article 4 covers contributions and insurance. For a member in an employment relationship the general rule is the social security and insurance regime of employees: INPS contributions paid by the cooperative, INAIL insurance. Some sectors have particular rules, to be checked case by case. For more, read [pension contributions when working through a cooperative](/en/articles/pension-contributions-working-through-cooperative/).

## Article 5: the link between the two relationships

Article 5 explains how the two relationships affect each other. The key point for members is paragraph 2: the working relationship ends when the member withdraws or is expelled, in line with the by-laws and the Civil Code. In other words, membership is the basis of the working relationship.

The same article contains rules on how labour law applies and on some procedural matters, such as which court handles disputes. If you are thinking of leaving, the practical side is in [leaving the cooperative](/en/articles/leaving-the-cooperative/).

## Article 6: the internal regulation

Article 6 requires cooperatives to adopt an internal regulation, approved by the members' meeting and filed with the competent office. Among other things, it must state the types of working relationship the cooperative intends to use with members, refer to the applicable collective agreements and describe how work is organised.

The regulation may also contain special provisions for specific situations, such as a business crisis, always within the limits and conditions set by law. To learn how to read it clause by clause, see [reading the internal regulation](/en/cooperation/reading-the-internal-regulation/).

## Fictional example: Silvia, project manager

Fictional example: imagine Silvia, a project manager with a Dutch client, who receives the paperwork to join a cooperative. Instead of reading everything in one go, she uses the law as an index. Article 1: she checks in the contract that the proposed relationship is employment. Article 3: she checks which collective agreement is referred to and how the by-laws describe patronage refunds. Article 4: she looks at how contributions and INAIL appear on a sample payslip. Article 5: she reads the grounds for withdrawal and expulsion in the by-laws. Article 6: she opens the regulation and looks for the types of relationship and how work is organised. In half an hour she has a clear picture and a list of questions.

## How it works with the cooperative

1. **Documents upfront.** {{< brand >}} provides the by-laws and regulation before admission.
2. **Admission.** Application, share capital, acceptance of the rules (art. 1).
3. **Employment contract.** Referring to the collective agreement and regulation (arts. 3 and 6).
4. **Payslip.** Monthly, with contributions and insurance (art. 4).
5. **Financial statements and members' meeting.** Where any patronage refunds are decided (art. 3, para. 2).

## Worth remembering

The law has been amended over time and some parts have been interpreted by the courts. This map follows the text in general terms: for concrete decisions, always check the current text and seek advice.
